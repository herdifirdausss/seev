### 0.2 One-paragraph conclusion

Under the `local-small` profile, three of the four canonical scenarios (S1
normal P2P, S3 webhook burst, S4 mixed journey) have a fully validated MSSL —
three independent 15-minute confirmations, one spike, and one 60-minute soak
at 70% MSSL, all passing with zero or near-zero dropped iterations and clean
ledger integrity. The fourth, S2 (hot-account), passed three 15-minute
confirmations and a spike at 120 WU/s cleanly, but its required 60-minute
soak at 84 WU/s (70% of that MSSL) failed severely — 6,556 dropped iterations
and a p99 of 2.8 seconds — while PostgreSQL's own container memory climbed
monotonically from 11% to 62% of its limit over the hour with no plateau.
This is not a lock-contention signal: a same-load re-run at the scenario's
original 160 WU/s knee point reproduced cleanly with a p99 two orders of
magnitude lower than the original observation, meaning the very first
"hot-account contention" signal found during discovery (Phase 2) was itself
not reproducible and should not be trusted. The soak failure's root cause is **confirmed**, not speculative: code review
plus an empirical 15-minute repro (tracking `pg_stat_user_tables` before,
during, and after) show `vendor_callback_inbox` and `payin_webhook_events`
both grow by exactly one row per webhook delivery, with zero deduplication,
because the load scenario mints a unique event ID on every delivery against
a small, reused reference pool, and neither table has a purge job anywhere
in the codebase. No connection, goroutine, or transaction leak exists in
vendor-service's own Go code — every pool setting, `rows.Close()`,
`defer cancel()`, and transaction boundary was checked and is correct. No
ledger, balance, idempotency, or outbox discrepancies were found in any
passing run. The B1-relevant soak finding is a confirmed platform-wide
data-model gap rather than account-level lock contention, unrelated to
which account the money settles against — and this session went on to
directly test B1 anyway: six alternating one-account/two-account runs at
S2's own confirmed 120 WU/s MSSL, with `cmd/loadprobe` extended to sample
lock waits specifically. Lock-wait share never exceeded 2.24% against a 20%
activation threshold, and the split-account variant was measurably *worse*
on average (0.44% lower throughput, more than double the p95), not better.
B1 is REJECT on a real negative result now, not on absence of a test. B3
(later session) is REJECT the same way: a real `CachingFeeRepository`
test-double against the real fee-quote resolver measured a 99.5% cache hit
rate — far above its own 80% cacheability threshold — yet produced
statistically zero throughput change and *worse* p95/p99, because the
resolver's ~0.06ms cost is negligible against ~3ms total request latency at
the tested load. B2 (later session) is REJECT the same evidenced way: a
real D0→D1→D2 growth-curve study on a schema-valid, verified-balanced
bulk-loaded dataset found linear ~484 bytes/entry growth and stable
sub-millisecond indexed queries through 5,000,000 entries, extrapolating to
an unremarkable ~19.4GB at the 40M-row activation gate — no size- or
query-degradation symptom to justify partitioning, on top of dataset scale
already being three-plus orders of magnitude below threshold.

revisited far more than it was in any shorter run — a candidate contributing
factor to §16.2's finding, not yet confirmed as the root cause.

## 10. Load Methodology

### 10.1 Run phases

Every run in this report went through: reset (fresh disposable Compose
project per run) → preflight integrity → `setup()` self-seed → steady
measurement window → stop ingress → outbox drain → post-run integrity →
artifact hashing. Matches the template's §10.1 phase list.

### 10.2 Discovery staircase (Phase 2, 2026-07-30)

Two passes were run, not one:

1. **Mini staircase** (15s/stage, explicitly non-canonical): 1, 2, 5, 10, 20,
   40, 80 WU/s across all five scenario scripts (W1–W5). Used only to find a
   *candidate* knee quickly. Two of its four findings were later proven
   **wrong** by the canonical pass:
   - W1 (S1): mini-pass reported clean-to-80 WU/s. Canonical (2-minute hold)
     found a real knee at 80 (228 dropped iterations) — a false negative.
   - W4 (S4): mini-pass reported a knee at 80 WU/s (15 dropped iterations).
     Canonical found 80 WU/s completely clean and the real knee at 100 — a
     false positive, caused by a handful of ramp-up drops dominating a small
     15-second sample.
2. **Canonical staircase** (2-minute hold per stage, K9): run for all five
   scenarios, narrowing each mini-pass signal. Results: W3 (payout,
   diagnostic-only) knee 20–30 WU/s; W4 knee 80–100 WU/s; W1 extended search
   found a knee at 80 but it did not reproduce consistently (0, 85, 228
   dropped iterations across three repeats at the same rate — a soft,
   marginal boundary, not a hard wall); W2/W5 pushed past 80 without finding
   a ceiling (W2 clean to 240, W5 clean to 120 with one severe but
   **non-reproducible** failure at 160 — see §16.2).

Both passes are **non-canonical relative to the K9 protocol's own 2-minute
hold** for the mini-pass, and the whole staircase phase is superseded in
authority by Phase 3 (§10.3) wherever the two disagree — which, per the
above, was most of the time. **No staircase number in this section should be
read as a capacity claim; only §11's confirmed numbers should.**

### 10.3 Confirmation, spike, and soak (Phase 3, 2026-07-31)

## 13. Resource and Saturation Evidence

### 13.1 Resource-at-load table

See §3 for the full peak-CPU/peak-memory table. The one saturation signal of
note: PostgreSQL and vendor-service memory both climbed **without plateauing**
across S2's full 60-minute soak (11%→62% and ~13%→~42% of their respective
container limits) while RabbitMQ (94.8%) and Prometheus (99.8%) were both
already near their own memory caps by the end of that same run — none of
these three were confirmed as the proximate cause of the dropped iterations,
but all three are evidence that the `local-small` profile's memory headroom
is thin at sustained high-volume webhook rates, independent of the specific
leak.

### 13.2 PostgreSQL evidence

`postgres-summary.jsonl` (`cmd/loadprobe`, 1s interval, capped at a 10-minute
window per invocation — **a real evidence gap for 60-minute soaks**, see
§24.4) was captured for every run. No `pg_locks`/blocked-session evidence
was found during any *passing* run (`blocked_locks: 0`, `waiting_queries`
low single digits throughout). The probe's own 10-minute window means it
never covered the back 50 minutes of any soak, including S2's failure — the
PostgreSQL-side evidence for §16.2 is `docker stats` memory/PID growth only,
not query-level evidence, and that gap is the single biggest reason §16.2's
root cause remains "suspected," not "confirmed."

### 13.3 Asynchronous pipeline

Outbox drain (`wait_for_outbox_drain`) reported `drain_seconds` at or near 0
for every passing run, i.e., outbox work was fully drained before the
post-run integrity check ran in every case. S2's failed soak's summary
carries `drain_seconds: 0` and `integrity_passed: false` — but that
`false` reflects that the run **never reached** the drain/verification
phase at all (k6 itself exited non-zero on the threshold breach, and
`load-test.sh`'s failure path tears down before drain/verify runs), not
that verification was attempted and failed. This distinction matters: S2's
soak failure is a throughput/latency finding, not a demonstrated financial
correctness failure.

## 15. Resource-to-Throughput Capacity Study

### 15.1 Scope actually run, and its real limitations

A real, live `local-2c-2g`/`local-8c-8g` comparison was run (later session),
but its scope is narrower than a canonical Phase 3 study, and one of its own
findings undercuts a clean three-way read against `local-small` — both are
stated plainly here rather than glossed over:

- **The harness had no working resource-profile enforcement at all before
  this session.** `deploy/load/profiles/*.yaml` was a self-reported,
  `cmd/loadcheck`-validated declaration completely disconnected from
  `deploy/load/compose.load.yaml`'s actual per-container limits — every
  service had a hardcoded memory limit and **no CPU limit whatsoever**,
  identical regardless of which profile was named.
  `pkg/loadlab.Profile.Validate()` additionally hard-rejected any profile
  whose `logical_cpus`/`docker_memory_mib` weren't exactly local-small's own
  4/4096 — so no other profile could ever have passed validation even if one
  had been written. Both are now fixed: `compose.load.yaml`'s memory limits
  are parameterized per service (defaults unchanged, verified byte-identical
  to before), `postgres` and `ledger-service` specifically now carry a real
  `cpus` limit (chosen because every prior bottleneck finding in this report
  points to these two as capacity-determining; every other service stays
  uncapped, as before), and `Validate()` now checks internal
  self-consistency instead of one hardcoded envelope.
- **Only two of ~13 services are CPU-capped** (postgres, ledger-service) —
  `local-small` itself has **no CPU cap on anything**. This is not a clean
  apples-to-apples three-profile comparison; it specifically isolates
  "what happens if postgres+ledger-service's own CPU is constrained,"
  nothing more.
- **Scope was deliberately lighter than canonical Phase 3 rigor**: three
  short (1-minute) samples per profile at 10/20/40 WU/s (W1 P2P), not a full
  staircase-discovery-confirm-spike-soak sequence — a genuine trade-off made
  explicit with the user rather than spending the ~6-8 hours full rigor on
  both new profiles would require.
- **This session hit real, unrelated infrastructure failures while running
  this study** that are worth recording since they cost most of the elapsed
  time and could recur: Docker Desktop's own VM crashed and restarted mid-run
  (wiping the build cache, ~21GB → ~445MB); the disposable stack's dev mTLS
  certificates (`deploy/certs`, 3-day validity) expired mid-session and had
  to be regenerated (`make certs`); a stale `mkdir`-based run lock directory
  from an earlier killed process blocked all subsequent runs until manually
  removed; and, the most consequential, a real bug in
  `scripts/load-test.sh`'s `start_postgres_probe` — `go run ./cmd/loadprobe
  ... &` backgrounds the `go run` *wrapper*, not the compiled binary it
  spawns as its own child, so `stop_background_collectors`' `kill
  "$POSTGRES_PROBE_PID"` killed the wrapper while the real probe process
  (and its `load_observer` database connection) survived, orphaned,
  sometimes for hours, at least once holding a connection open against a
  database `compose down -v` was concurrently trying to tear down. Fixed by
  pre-building the binary and backgrounding it directly, so `$!` is the real
  worker's pid; `cmd/loaddataset` and `cmd/loadprobe`'s own per-sample query
  contexts were also unbounded (`context.Background()`, no timeout) and
  could have compounded the same failure mode independently — both now use a
  bounded context.

### 15.3 What the data actually shows

**Two findings, not one, and they point in different directions:**

1. **Capped vs. uncapped is a large, real difference.** At the identical 20
   WU/s offered rate, `local-small` (uncapped) achieved 95.5% with p95=10ms;
   both capped profiles achieved ~57% with p95≈265ms — over 25x worse
   tail latency. Constraining postgres+ledger-service's CPU at all, even to
   4 cores, costs real capacity and latency relative to no limit.

2. **1 vCPU vs. 4 vCPU (2c-2g vs. 8c-8g) made essentially no difference** —
   achieved % and p50 are within noise of each other at every offered rate,
   and p95/p99 are likewise close. Quadrupling the CPU cap on the two
   services this report's own prior findings (§16, §20) point to as
   capacity-determining produced no measurable improvement. Two explanations
   are both consistent with the evidence and this experiment cannot
   distinguish between them:
   - postgres+ledger-service genuinely were not the binding constraint in
     this range — something uncapped (another service, k6's own
     `preAllocatedVUs`, mTLS connection setup) was already the ceiling
     before 1 CPU became limiting, which finding 1 alone doesn't rule out
     (the drop from uncapped could be from the *existence* of a cap
     changing scheduling behavior, not from 1 CPU specifically being too
     little); or
   - 1 CPU and 4 CPUs are simply both comfortably above what this specific
     low-absolute-load scenario (10-40 WU/s of simple P2P transfers) needs,
     so neither capped run was anywhere near ITS OWN ceiling — consistent
     with the near-identical achieved-% pattern repeating at every offered
     rate rather than diverging as load increases, which is what a genuine
     CPU-bound difference between 1 and 4 cores would be expected to do.
   
   Distinguishing these needs either a higher offered rate than 40 WU/s
   (not tested — the ~58% achieved ceiling repeating across all three
   offered rates suggests the constraint is proportional to offered load
   rather than a fixed capacity number, which itself is a signal something
   client-side, like k6's own VU allocation, is the actual limit, not
   server-side CPU at all) or capping more of the ~13 services than just
   these two.

rate per profile, not a confirmed MSSL — see §15.2's caveat):

| Profile | WU/s (at 40 offered) | WU/s / vCPU | WU/s / GiB |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `local-2c-2g` | 23.19 | 11.6 | 11.6 |
| `local-8c-8g` | 22.82 | 2.85 | 2.85 |
| `local-small` (confirmed MSSL 20, uncapped, 4 vCPU/4 GiB reference) | 19.10 | 4.78 | 4.78 |

The 2c-2g profile's higher WU/s/vCPU is an artifact of finding 2 (near-zero
marginal throughput from the extra 3 capped CPUs), not evidence that less
CPU is genuinely more efficient — dividing an unchanged numerator by a
larger denominator mechanically produces a smaller ratio.

### 15.4 Conclusion

This resolves less than a canonical Phase 5 would: it demonstrates *that*
CPU constraints on postgres/ledger-service matter (real, large effect vs.
uncapped) but does not cleanly demonstrate elasticity *within* the tested
2-8 vCPU range for this scenario, because the achieved-throughput pattern is
consistent with the actual constraint being something other than
postgres/ledger CPU at these offered rates. A future, more conclusive study
would need: (a) a higher offered-rate ceiling per profile (push until each
profile's own knee, not a fixed 10/20/40 sweep), and (b) capping enough of
the remaining ~11 services to be confident the constraint under test is
actually server-side CPU and not `k6`'s own concurrency limits or an
unconstrained service elsewhere in the chain.

# D. Bottleneck Explanation

## 16. Bottleneck Analysis Template

### 16.1 S1, S3, S4 — soft, low-margin ceilings

None of S1, S3, or S4 showed a sharp, single-resource knee. Each scenario's
confirmed MSSL sits at a rate where CPU (§3) and pool evidence (not
separately captured this session — see §24.4) still had headroom, but
sustained (15-minute) runs at the *next* staircase rung up consistently
produced small, non-zero `dropped_iterations` counts (tens to low hundreds
out of tens of thousands of iterations) that never appeared in the 2-minute
canonical staircase at the same rate. This pattern — clean at 2 minutes,
marginal at 15 minutes — repeated identically for S1 (60→marginal, 40→
marginal, 20→clean×3) and S4 (80→severe, 40→marginal, 20→clean×3), and once
for S3 (160→marginal, 120→marginal, 80→clean×3). No single saturated
resource (CPU, pool, lock, disk, broker) was isolated as the cause; the most
defensible read is generator/executor timing sensitivity compounding over a
longer window, not a genuine backend capacity wall. This should be treated
as a methodology caveat for any future re-run on the same profile, not as a
description of backend behavior.

### 16.2 S2 — PostgreSQL/vendor-service memory growth (soak-only failure) — ROOT CAUSE CONFIRMED

#### Where did throughput stop scaling?

- Last healthy offered load (15-minute confirmation): 120 WU/s, 3/3 clean.
- First unhealthy offered load: **not a rate** — 84 WU/s (30% *below* the
  confirmed 120 WU/s MSSL) failed only at 60-minute duration. Three
  15-minute confirmations and one 3-minute spike at rates from 20 through
  210 WU/s were all clean.
- This rules out a rate-driven capacity knee for S2 and points at a
  **duration-driven** resource issue instead.

#### What resource or dependency saturated first?

Evidence gathered (see §13.2 for the gap in this evidence):

- PostgreSQL container memory: 72 MB → 396 MB (11% → 62% of its 640 MiB
  limit), monotonic, no plateau, across the full 60 minutes.
- PostgreSQL backend process count (PIDs): 17 → 26–29, never returning to
  baseline — consistent with connections accumulating rather than being
  returned to a pool.
- vendor-service container memory: 24 MB → 76 MB (13% → 40% of its 192 MiB
  limit) over the same window — the only application service whose memory
  also grew substantially, making it the leading candidate for *where* a
  leak originates (it receives every webhook delivery directly), though this
  is not confirmed.
- No `pg_locks`/blocked-session evidence exists for the back 50 minutes of
  this run (the probe's fixed 10-minute window, §13.2), so lock contention
  cannot be ruled in or out directly from that source — but the confirmed
  root cause below fully accounts for the failure without invoking locks.
- **This is not the K11 hot-account signal it first appeared to be.** The
  original Phase 2 discovery flagged 160 WU/s as a severe contention point
  (318 unexpected failures, 16.5s p99). A same-rate, same-duration (2-minute)
  re-run produced **zero** unexpected failures and a p99 of 160 ms — two
  orders of magnitude lower. That original finding does not reproduce and
  should not be cited as evidence of lock contention.

#### Root cause (confirmed by code review + empirical verification)

A code-level investigation (`internal/vendorboundary/callback.go`,
`internal/payin/repository/repository.go`) ruled out every classic Go
resource-leak pattern on this request path: the `sql.DB` pool is correctly
configured (`MaxOpenConns=10`, `MaxIdleConns=5`, `ConnMaxLifetime=5m`,
`internal/config/config.go:590-593`); every `QueryContext` call has its
`rows.Close()`; every `context.WithTimeout`/`WithCancel` has its
`defer cancel()`; `CallbackHandler.ServeHTTP` never opens a transaction, so
no connection is held across the downstream gRPC call to payin-service; and
no per-request goroutine, cache, or connection was found anywhere in
vendor-service's own code.

**The actual cause is a data-model gap, not a leak in the classic sense.**
`InboxStore.Ensure` (`internal/vendorboundary/callback.go:279-282`) inserts
into `vendor_callback_inbox` on `ON CONFLICT (vendor, vendor_event_id) DO
NOTHING` — but `tests/load/lib/seed.js`'s `deliverWebhook()` mints a
**unique `event_id` on every single delivery** while reusing the **same
pre-created intent's external reference** (capped at 200 intents per
`seedHotspotPool`, §4.1). The conflict key never matches, so **every
delivery inserts a brand-new row**, followed by two more `UPDATE`s
(`claim`, `Finish`). The identical pattern repeats one hop downstream in
`payin_webhook_events` (`internal/payin/repository/repository.go:106-132`).
Neither table has a purge/retention job anywhere in the codebase (the only
retention mechanism found, `fn_retention_purge_webhook_events_raw`, only
NULLs an encrypted column after 30 days — rows and their indexes live
forever).

**Empirically verified** with a dedicated 15-minute repro at the same 84
WU/s soak rate, sampling `pg_stat_user_tables` before/during/after:

| Time | `vendor_callback_inbox` rows | Table size | `payin_webhook_events` rows | Table size | Postgres container memory |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 min (baseline) | 0 | 40 KB | 0 | 48 KB | — |
| 5 min | 24,514 | 28 MB | 24,475 | 22 MB | — |
| 10 min | 50,879 | 53 MB | 50,988 | 46 MB | 36.23% |
| 15 min (final) | 75,598 | 82 MB | 75,656 | 71 MB | 38.05% |

Insert counts track offered rate almost exactly (75,601 inserts at 84 WU/s ×
900s ≈ 75,600 expected) — confirming zero deduplication. The
`vendor_callback_inbox_vendor_vendor_event_id_key` unique index alone reached
15 MB in 15 minutes — every insert must probe this index for the (never-hit)
conflict check, and it is scanned against the same small pool of 200 external
references repeatedly (a hot-index-page pattern). Postgres's own memory
growth in this 15-minute repro (243.5 MiB / 38.05% at minute 15) matches the
original 60-minute soak's memory at the same elapsed time (224.2 MiB / 35.03%
at minute 15) almost exactly, confirming the correlation is causal and
reproducible, not coincidental. Autovacuum did run during the repro window
and reclaimed most `vendor_callback_inbox` dead tuples (391 remaining vs. a
potential 151,202 from the 2-updates-per-row churn) — but autovacuum reclaims
*dead* space, not *live* row count, so it cannot stop the underlying growth.

**Therefore the confirmed first-order cause is unbounded row insertion in
`vendor_callback_inbox` and `payin_webhook_events`, driven by the load
scenario's realistic-but-aggressive redelivery pattern (unique `event_id`
per delivery against a small, reused reference pool) combined with the
absence of any purge/retention job for either table** — not CPU, not
connection-pool exhaustion, and not row-lock contention on the shared
settlement account. vendor-service's own container memory growth (§3, §13.1)
is best explained as a symptom (requests piling up in-flight as Postgres
queries slow down under the growing table/index), not an independent leak —
no evidence of one was found in its own code.

One important caveat on severity, **not** on the root cause itself: the load
scenario's redelivery rate (~1,479 unique-event-id deliveries against the
same reference over the hour) is far more aggressive than typical production
webhook-vendor retry behavior (usually a small, fixed number of retries with
backoff). This means the specific *speed* at which this session hit the
failure is a synthetic worst case, not a claim that production would fail
within an hour at similar traffic. The underlying gap — no retention job,
webhooks against an already-settled intent still writing new
`vendor_callback_inbox`/`payin_webhook_events` rows — is real regardless of
traffic shape and would surface in production over a period of months to
years rather than an hour, absent a fix.

#### Evidence chain

At 84 WU/s sustained for 60 minutes (S2, variant `one`), throughput held for
the first ~20–30 minutes then began dropping iterations, reaching 6,556
dropped and 321 unexpected failures with p99 rising to 2.8 seconds by the
end of the hour. A dedicated 15-minute repro at the identical rate confirmed
`vendor_callback_inbox` and `payin_webhook_events` both grow by one row per
webhook delivery with zero deduplication (75,601 inserts for 75,601 offered
iterations), reaching 82 MB and 71 MB respectively with no purge job to bound
them, and PostgreSQL's container memory growth curve in that repro matched
the original 60-minute soak's curve at the same elapsed time almost exactly.
Code review ruled out every classic connection/goroutine/transaction leak
pattern in vendor-service. **Therefore the confirmed cause is unbounded,
write-amplified row growth from a missing idempotent-no-write short-circuit
and a missing retention/purge job — a data-model gap, not a resource leak in
vendor-service's own Go code, and not row-lock contention on the shared
settlement account.**

The same growth *pattern* (10%→36.5% PostgreSQL memory) appeared in S4's
soak at 14 WU/s, at roughly one-sixth the total event volume (50,401 vs.
295,845 iterations) and stayed well under the failure threshold — consistent
with the growth being driven by cumulative event volume, not wall-clock
duration alone, and with the same root cause (S4's mixed journey includes a
20% topup+webhook share hitting the same tables).

#### Fix applied and verified (post-report, same session)

A follow-up investigation (prompted by "is there a durable audit trail
elsewhere that would make deleting these rows safe?") found the actual gap
one level deeper than "no purge job exists": **`vendor-service` was the only
one of nine owner services never wired into the platform's own
`retentionworker` framework at all.** Every other service (ledger, auth,
payin, payout, fraud, gateway, adminbff, assurance) already had a
`<owner>_retention_holds`/`<owner>_retention_audit` migration and a
`StartRetentionRunner` registered against a daily cron; `config/data-retention.yaml`
already *declared* `vendor.callback_inbox` (redact @30d) and
`vendor.outbound_attempts` (retain_permanent) policy, but nothing ever
executed it — a wiring gap, not a missing design decision. A companion
schema audit found the identical gap also left a real concurrency bug live:
`vendor_outbound_attempts`' insert (`internal/vendorboundary/service.go`,
`serverRecordOutbound`) computed its next `attempt` number via
`COALESCE(MAX(attempt),0)+1` and silently discarded the error
(`_, _ = db.ExecContext(...)`) — two concurrent calls for the same
`(flow, request_id, operation)` (e.g. overlapping `QueryPayout` polls) could
collide on that table's own unique constraint and lose a row from what
policy declares a permanent audit trail.

Both were fixed and verified in this session, following the exact pattern
every other service already uses (not a new design):

- `migrations/vendor/000002_retention_holds.up.sql` /
  `000003_retention_purge_callback_inbox.up.sql` add
  `vendor_retention_holds`/`vendor_retention_audit` and a
  `fn_retention_purge_callback_inbox_raw` redact function; `internal/vendorboundary/retention.go`
  registers it and `cmd/vendor-service/main.go` starts it; `vendor` was
  added to `pkg/retentionworker/schedule.go`'s jitter schedule.
  `go run ./cmd/retentioncheck` went from 2 policy violations to
  104/104 valid. **Verified against a live disposable stack**: a dry-run
  call correctly counted exactly the one eligible (terminal, >30-day-old)
  row seeded for the test; a real call redacted `raw_body`/`selected_headers`
  to `{"redacted":true}` while leaving every correlation column
  (`vendor`, `vendor_event_id`, `external_reference`, `amount`, `currency`,
  `processing_status`, timestamps) untouched, and wrote a correct
  `vendor_retention_audit` row for both calls.
- `serverRecordOutbound` now retries up to 3 times on a duplicate-key error
  (`pkg/generalerror.IsDuplicateKey`) before logging a failure, instead of
  silently discarding it.
- Three amount columns found missing a positivity `CHECK` during the same
  schema audit (`fraud.screening_events.amount`, `ledger.recon_items.amount`,
  `assurance_findings.amount_minor`) were also closed, each verified safe by
  reading every insert path first (all three already validate positivity in
  application code; the constraint is a DB-level backstop, not a behavior
  change).

**What this fix does and does not change about §16.2's finding.** It closes
a genuine policy-compliance gap (vendor's raw payloads are now redacted
after 30 days like every other service's) and a genuine correctness bug
(the swallowed insert error). It does **not** change the row-count-growth
characteristic itself — redaction nulls payload content, it does not delete
rows, and deletion remains explicitly forbidden by
`docs/roadmap/archive/51-a8-data-lifecycle-privacy.md` and
`config/data-retention.yaml` for both `vendor_callback_inbox` and
`payin_webhook_events` (both are classified permanent financial/reconciliation
evidence, and `payin_topup_intents.settled_event_id` holds a `RESTRICT`
foreign key into `payin_webhook_events` besides). A re-run of the exact S2
soak that failed would very likely still fail today, for the same reason —
this fix brings vendor-service into policy compliance and fixes a real
audit-integrity bug, but the unbounded row/index growth remains accepted,
by-design behavior that only capacity planning (B2/partitioning, once real
scale approaches its activation gate) or a future, deliberate policy change
authorizing deletion could address. §25 is updated accordingly.

### 16.3 B1 experiment — real evidence, not "insufficient evidence" (post-report, same session)

Phase 4's B1 protocol (variant `two`, alternating A/B, lock-wait sampling)
was run for the first time this session, closing the gap §20 originally
flagged. `cmd/loadprobe` was first extended to sample
`wait_event_type = 'Lock'` specifically (the prior probe only counted any
`wait_event`, which includes non-lock waits like I/O or client) and to
report which relation/lock-mode pairs are contended
(`cmd/loadprobe/main.go`'s new `LockWaitRelations`), since the prior probe
had no way to answer B1's "concentration on the same system-account
dependency" criterion at all. `scripts/load-test.sh`'s probe duration cap
was also raised from 10m to 2h (it was silently truncating coverage on any
run over 10 minutes, discovered in §13.2).

Six runs, alternating one/two/one/two/one/two, each at 120 WU/s (S2's
confirmed MSSL) for 3 minutes, same profile and dataset shape throughout —
only the variant differs:

- Variant `two` (the split-account control) achieved **0.44% lower**

- Variant `two`'s average p95 was **more than double** variant `one`'s

  transient DDL-like activity (e.g. autovacuum's truncate phase) coinciding
  with variant `two`'s heavier `setup()` (which registers a second user and
  writes a payin routing override) than with per-transaction row contention
  on the shared account.

**This is a materially different and stronger conclusion than "insufficient
evidence": B1 was actually tested, at its own confirmed MSSL, with proper
lock-wait instrumentation, and the shared-account condition showed no
meaningful lock contention and no throughput/latency benefit from
splitting — if anything, the split variant was measurably worse, plausibly
from its own extra per-run setup cost rather than any genuine account-level
effect.** §19/§20 are updated to reflect this as an evidence-based finding.

### 16.4 A different hot-account shape: batch/burst disbursement (post-report, same session)

A user question surfaced a real gap in §16.3's scope: B1's experiment tested
*steady* webhook traffic (a constant arrival rate) against a shared account.
It said nothing about a *batch/burst* pattern — e.g. an end-of-day payroll
or disbursement cutoff releasing many transactions concentrated in a short
window, all touching one account. This platform has exactly that feature
(`disbursement_batches`/`disbursement_items`,
`migrations/ledger/000015_disbursement.up.sql`): every item, in every batch,
regardless of which batch, debits the same singleton
`settlement[platform][currency]` account — a different account and a
different code path (`internal/ledger/service/disbursement`) than W5/B1's
`settlement[gateway]` accounts, and one no existing W1–W7 scenario touches
at all.

**A genuine routing bug was discovered while building this test.** The
"obvious" client path, `/api/v1/admin/ledger/disbursements` (also what
admin-bff's own proxy uses, `internal/adminbff/module.go:117`), 404s. Cause:
`cmd/ledger-service/main.go:338` mounts admin routes as
`api.Handle("/api/v1/admin/ledger/", authed(http.StripPrefix("/api/v1", module.InternalRouter())))`
— `StripPrefix` removes only `"/api/v1"` (7 characters), leaving
`/admin/ledger/disbursements` for a mux that only registers
`/admin/disbursements` (no `/ledger` segment,
`internal/ledger/transport/http.go:194-196`). Every existing test for these
handlers (`internal/ledger/transport/http_test.go`) calls the inner mux
directly with its own un-prefixed paths, so this mount was never actually
exercised end-to-end by anything in the repository. **This affects every
route under this mount, not just disbursements** — adjustments, recon,
savings, schedules, and outbox-admin are mounted the same way and are
therefore equally unreachable via `/api/v1/admin/ledger/*`, including
through admin-bff's real production proxy. The working path (verified live)
is `/api/v1/ledger/admin/*` instead — the *other* mount at
`cmd/ledger-service/main.go:337` strips its own full matched prefix
correctly. This is a real product bug, found as a side effect of building
this test, not something this report's own load-generator code caused —
flagged here for a fix separate from this performance work.

#### Routing bug fixed and verified (post-report, later session)

The root cause traced one level deeper than "wrong `StripPrefix` length":
there is **no** `StripPrefix` value that makes
`/api/v1/admin/ledger/disbursements` reach
`internal/ledger/transport/http.go`'s `/admin/disbursements` pattern,
because the URL's `admin`/`ledger` segment order has no counterpart in
`InternalRouter()`'s route table (which is all `/admin/*`, never
`/admin/ledger/*`) — any contiguous prefix strip either leaves the stray
`/ledger` segment in, or strips it along with `/admin`, which no route
matches either. The only mount that *does* strip cleanly
(`/api/v1/ledger/` → `/admin/*`, `cmd/ledger-service/main.go:337`) was
already correct; the broken second mount at line 338 was therefore not
fixable in place — it was removed. admin-bff's proxy
(`internal/adminbff/module.go:117`) was repointed to target the working
mount instead, using the exact rewrite convention its own adjustments/recon
proxies already used correctly (`publicPrefix` unchanged, `downstreamPrefix`
rewritten from `/api/v1/admin/ledger/` to `/api/v1/ledger/admin/`). This
also fixes savings, schedules, and outbox-admin, all mounted the same way,
and the disbursement approve/reject endpoints added later the same session
(business-completeness audit finding — bulk disbursement now requires a
second identity's approval before `Run`).

The path-rewrite arithmetic was extracted into a small pure function
(`rewriteProxyPath`, `internal/adminbff/proxy.go`) and unit-tested directly:
`/api/v1/admin/ledger/disbursements` now maps to
`/api/v1/ledger/admin/disbursements`, matching the adjustments/recon
routes' own already-correct behavior. **This is a mechanical/unit-test
verification, not a repeat of the earlier live-stack verification this
report used for the S2 leak fix** — the fix was not re-confirmed against a
running disposable stack in this pass.

Two burst runs, each creating N batches of 100 items in `setup()` then
firing all N `POST .../run` calls at a high k6 rate over a 2-second window
(maximizing concurrent in-flight calls against the shared account):

| Run | Batches (N) | Total iterations | Dropped | p95 | p99 | Lock-wait samples |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| burst1 | 40 | 40 | 0 | 313 ms | 405 ms | 0 / 39 |
| burst2 | 80 | 81 | 0 | 483 ms | 697 ms | 0 / 46 |

Both runs completed with zero dropped iterations, zero unexpected failures,
and clean post-run ledger integrity. `cmd/loadprobe`'s lock-wait sampling
(the same instrumentation built for §16.3) recorded **zero** samples with
any session in a `Lock` wait state across both runs, despite p99 latency
roughly doubling from the 40-batch to the 80-batch run (405 ms → 697 ms) —
that latency growth is consistent with real serialization pressure on the
shared account (each batch's 100 items post strictly sequentially per
`internal/ledger/service/disbursement/disbursement.go`'s plain `for` loop,
confirmed via code review), just not one that a 1-second sampling interval
reliably caught in the act. **This is a genuine finding, but a narrower one
than "no contention exists": no *sustained* lock contention was detected at
1-second sampling resolution under this burst shape, at this scale (up to
81 concurrent-ish batch runs). It does not rule out brief,
sub-second contention that this sampling interval is simply too coarse to
observe** — closing that gap would need either finer-grained sampling or
direct `pg_stat_statements`/instrumented timing on the specific balance
`UPDATE`, neither done here.

## 20. B1 — Hot-Account Sub-Sharding

### Decision

**REJECT** — a real negative result, not "insufficient evidence" (post-report
update: the experiment described as not-yet-run below was subsequently
executed this same session — see §16.3).

### Rationale

B1's activation criteria (≥20% lock-wait share, ≥80% same-account
concentration, a controlled two-gateway A/B showing ≥25% throughput or ≥30%
p95 gain, CPU <70%, pool <80%, ≥3 alternating runs) were directly measured:
six alternating one-account/two-account runs at S2's own confirmed 120 WU/s
MSSL, with `cmd/loadprobe` extended to sample `wait_event_type = 'Lock'`
specifically and report which relation is contended (§16.3). Results:
lock-wait share never exceeded 2.24% in any run (average 0.32% for the
shared-account variant, 1.07% for the split variant) — nowhere near the 20%
threshold. The split-account (`two`) variant did not improve throughput or
latency; it was measurably *worse* on average (0.44% lower throughput, more
than double the p95), the opposite of what activation would require. The
one large S2 finding this session otherwise produced — a 60-minute soak
failure — was separately root-caused (§16.2) to unbounded row growth in
`vendor_callback_inbox`/`payin_webhook_events`, a data-model gap unrelated
to which account the money settles against; it does not change this
section's conclusion.

### Consequence

Keep the single-account model. Nothing in this session's evidence supports
reopening B1 for this scenario/profile; a future reopening would need a
different, larger-scale signal (e.g. real production contention data) to
justify re-testing, not a re-run of this same experiment.

## 21. B2 — Partitioning

### Final decision

**REJECT** — now a directly-measured growth curve, later session, not just
"dataset far below threshold."

### Rationale

§21's own required study — "run the read/verifier suite at D0, D1, and D2;
collect database/table/index sizes and representative plans; calculate
actual growth slope" — was executed against a real, cumulatively-grown,
schema-valid `ledger_transactions`/`ledger_entries` dataset (double-entry
balanced by construction, confirmed via `fn_verify_ledger_balance` finding
zero unbalanced transactions across the full 5,000,000-entry result, and a
global debit/credit sum match). Built via direct bulk SQL against a
throwaway Postgres container with the real ledger migrations applied — not
through the HTTP API (self-seeding via real business journeys, as S1–S4
use, would take far too long at millions of rows; this is a schema/size
study, not a business-journey correctness study, and correctness of the
double-entry invariant itself was already separately proven, §16.2's own
balance trigger work).

| Tier | Entries | `ledger_entries` (table+index) | `ledger_transactions` (table+index) | Combined bytes/entry | Representative query (indexed, single-account lookup) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| D0 | 100,000 | 33 MB (10+23) | 14 MB (7.7+6.5) | ~493 B | 0.202 ms |
| D1 | 1,000,000 | 325 MB (104+221) | 134 MB (75+59) | ~481 B | 0.113 ms |
| D2 | 5,000,000 | 1,626 MB (521+1,105) | 681 MB (376+305) | ~484 B | 0.543 ms |

**Growth is linear and consistent** — ~480–493 bytes/entry (table+index
combined) held stable across a 50x scale range (100K→5M), no super-linear
blowup or index-bloat signature. **Query performance stayed comfortably
sub-millisecond throughout** (0.11–0.54 ms) and kept using the same index
scan strategy at every scale — no evidence of degradation that would itself
motivate partitioning on query-performance grounds.

**Extrapolating linearly to §21's own 40M-row activation gate**: 40,000,000
entries × ~484 bytes/entry ≈ **19.4 GB** combined table+index size — a
moderate, unremarkable size for a single Postgres table on any reasonably
provisioned instance, not a size that alone would force partitioning.
Per §15.5, this is a >2x extrapolation beyond the measured 5M-entry point
and should be treated as a hypothesis, not a validated forecast — but it is
directly in tension with treating 40M rows as inherently requiring
partitioning: the actual bottleneck partitioning solves (vacuum pressure,
retention/deletion pain, or a specific degraded query shape) was not
observed at any measured scale, and combined with dataset scale being three-plus
orders of magnitude below the threshold (self-seeded pools this session
ranged from 50–200 rows per business scenario), both of §21's own required
conditions — scale AND a measured symptom — are unmet.

## 22. B3 — Routing Cache

### Decision

**REJECT** — a real negative result, later session (post-report update: the
experiment described as not-yet-run below was subsequently executed).

### Rationale

The resolver: `feepolicy.Policy.Resolve` → `FeeRepository.ResolveRule`, the
SELECT `POST /api/v1/ledger/fees/quote` (w6-resolver.js, self-seeding added
this session) issues on every call — server-controlled fee pricing, keyed on
`(tx_type, currency, user_id, gateway)`, ordered by specificity. A B3
test-double (`internal/ledger/feepolicy/cache.go`'s `CachingFeeRepository`,
a TTL-memoized decorator, gated behind `FEE_RESOLVER_CACHE_TTL` —
unset/off by default, touching no existing behavior) was built and run
against the real endpoint at 40 WU/s / 2 minutes, alternating baseline
(no cache) and a 5s-TTL cached variant:

| Run | Resolver calls | Total iterations | Achieved WU/s | p50 | p95 | p99 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Baseline (no cache) | 4801 (1:1 with iterations) | 4801 | 39.89 | 2.99 ms | 6.93 ms | 15.84 ms |
| Cached (5s TTL) | 12 (verified via direct `pg_stat_statements` query, 1-min run, 2401 iterations) | 2401 | 39.89 | 2.74 ms | 7.31 ms | 16.23 ms |

**Repeated-key cacheability: 99.5%** (12 calls / 2401 iterations) — far
above the 80% threshold; the whole scenario deliberately reuses one
`(tx_type, currency, user_id, gateway)` key throughout, the shape real
per-user/per-route fee resolution would actually have.

**Resolver's share of database time is genuinely ambiguous at this scale**:
7.82% of *all* `pg_stat_statements` time sampled during the run (below the
15% threshold) — but that denominator is dominated by `cmd/loadprobe`'s own
1s-interval self-monitoring queries (`pg_stat_statements`/`pg_locks`/
`pg_database_size` self-reads, ~40% of sampled time — a measurement
artifact of a short run at fine sampling, not real application load) and
one-time migration/database-creation bootstrap cost. Restricted to just the
two queries the quote endpoint itself issues (the resolver SELECT and the
`fee_quotes` INSERT), the resolver is **40.1%** of that endpoint's own DB
time — comfortably above 15%. Neither denominator is unambiguously "the"
right one; this is flagged rather than resolved in the report's favor either
way.

**The deciding criterion is unambiguous and negative**: even at a ~99.5%
cache hit rate, achieved throughput was statistically identical between
runs (39.89 vs. 39.89 WU/s) and p95/p99 were *worse*, not better, with
caching (7.31 ms vs. 6.93 ms p95; 16.23 ms vs. 15.84 ms p99) — the opposite
of the required ≥15% throughput or ≥20% p95 improvement. The resolver's own
mean cost (0.057–0.063 ms per call, confirmed via `pg_stat_statements`) is
such a small fraction of the ~3 ms total request latency (network,
serialization, other endpoint work) that eliminating 99.5% of its calls is
undetectable end to end at this load level (40 WU/s, well under any
observed capacity ceiling for this endpoint). A cache cannot speed up a
request whose bottleneck was never the cached operation — this is the same
shape of result as B1 (§20): a plausible-sounding optimization with no
measured benefit once actually tested.

### Consequence

Retain the direct resolver path — `CachingFeeRepository` stays an
unused, off-by-default test-double (`internal/ledger/feepolicy/cache.go`),
not a shipped feature; activating it would add invalidation-on-write
complexity (§22's own "If activated" list) for a change with no measured
benefit. Reopening B3 would need evidence the resolver is contended at a
load level where its cost is no longer negligible relative to total request
latency — this session's `local-small`/40 WU/s test does not rule that out
at higher load, only at this one.
